# Real-world climate tipping points, +1.2 °C to +3.0 °C

Research for [ticket #61](https://github.com/whaleyjoshua2/Dying-Earth/issues/61), which feeds the Tipping Points ticket under the wayfinder map ([#49](https://github.com/whaleyjoshua2/Dying-Earth/issues/49)). It answers one question: which parts of the Earth system are thought to flip inside the band the game already runs in — Base Temperature +1.2 °C, Collapse Line +3.0 °C — at what warming, over what timescale, and with what physical consequence.

Everything below is cited. Where a number is my arithmetic rather than a source's, it says so.

## Where the numbers come from

The single best source is **Armstrong McKay et al. (2022), "Exceeding 1.5 °C global warming could trigger multiple climate tipping points", *Science* 377, eabn7950** — [doi:10.1126/science.abn7950](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abn7950). It is the first comprehensive reassessment of every proposed tipping element, and its Table 1 is the only published table that gives a threshold estimate with a minimum and maximum, a timescale, and an impact for all sixteen elements at once. Science's site is paywalled to automated fetching; the published article was read from a hosted copy of the same PDF at [krichlab.ca](https://krichlab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Science-Armstrong-Mckay-2022-Exceeding-1.5-oC-global-warming-could-trigger-multiple-climate-tipping-points.pdf), and Table 1 below is transcribed from it.

The **IPCC AR6** is the conservative counterweight. It is deliberately more cautious than the tipping-element literature: it assesses confidence rather than publishing threshold numbers, and for most elements it declines to name a temperature at all. AR6 WG1 collects its tipping assessment in **Section 4.7.2 and Table 4.10**, "Cross-chapter assessment ... of components in the Earth system that have been proposed as susceptible to tipping points/abrupt change" ([Chapter 4 PDF](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter04.pdf)). Note for anyone chasing this later: there is no "Box 4.1" on tipping points — Box 4.1 is about ensemble weighting.

The **Global Tipping Points Report 2025** (Lenton et al., University of Exeter) is the newest assessment and revises one threshold downward in a way that matters to this game; it is used where it disagrees with the 2022 paper ([global-tipping-points.org](https://global-tipping-points.org/)).

AR6's own definition, from Section 4.7.2: a tipping point is "a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganizes, often abruptly and/or irreversibly, and a tipping element ... a component of the Earth system that is susceptible to a tipping point" ([Chapter 4](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter04.pdf)).

## The full table

Timescale is the years the transition itself is thought to take once triggered. "Max. impact" is the element's own feedback on global mean surface temperature, unless a carbon or sea-level figure is given instead. Nine elements are classed **global core** (they change how the Earth system works); seven are **regional impact** (they matter to people or are irreplaceable, without reorganising the planet).

**Seven elements have a central estimate at or below +3.0 °C**, and every one of the sixteen except Arctic winter sea ice and the East Antarctic Ice Sheet has a *minimum* below +3.0 °C. The game's whole temperature range is inside the tipping zone.

## The elements that matter to this game, one by one

Each entry ends with a plain-English line short enough to sit on a card.

### Warm-water coral reefs — die-off

**+1.5 °C (1.0–2.0), ten years.** Armstrong McKay's estimate. The **Global Tipping Points Report 2025 revised this down**: "With current global warming at about 1.4 °C, reefs are passing their thermal tipping point (central estimate 1.2 °C, range 1–1.5 °C)" — that is, it is being crossed now, and the report calls warm-water coral "the first ecosystem on Earth to have passed a planetary-scale tipping point" ([National Oceanography Centre](https://www.noc.ac.uk/news/global-tipping-points-report-2025-reveals-first-earth-system-tipping-point-reached), [Stockholm Resilience Centre](https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/research-stories/2025-10-13-world-reaches-first-climate-tipping-point---widespread-mortality-of-coral-reefs.html)). IPCC SR1.5 SPM B.4.2 states that "Coral reefs, for example, are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5°C (high confidence) with larger losses (>99%) at 2°C (very high confidence)" ([SR1.5 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/sites/2/2022/06/SPM_version_report_LR.pdf)); AR6's Synthesis Report SPM carries those same two figures forward in Figure SPM.3 and states that above 1.5 °C warm-water coral reefs "will have reached or surpassed hard adaptation limits" ([SYR SPM B.4.2](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_SPM.pdf)).

**It does not feed back on warming.** Its cost is to about a billion people's food and coastal protection, and to a quarter of marine species. This is the one element that is unambiguously already going and unambiguously *not* a climate accelerator.

> *Card line:* The reefs bleach and do not come back; a quarter of everything in the sea loses its home.

### Boreal permafrost — abrupt thaw

**+1.5 °C (1.0–2.3), 200 years.** Permafrost holds about 1,035 GtC. Gradual thaw is a threshold-free feedback; *abrupt* thaw — slope slumping, thermokarst lakes — "could increase emissions by 50 to 100% relative to gradual thaw", is self-continuing once started, and "could occur near-synchronously on a subcontinental scale". The assessed extra is **~10 to 25 GtC per °C**, worth about +0.04 °C per °C by 2100 rising to +0.11 °C per °C by 2300 ([Armstrong McKay et al. 2022](https://krichlab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Science-Armstrong-Mckay-2022-Exceeding-1.5-oC-global-warming-could-trigger-multiple-climate-tipping-points.pdf)).

AR6 WG1 Chapter 5 puts the whole permafrost CO2 feedback at **18 [3.1 to 41] PgC per °C of global warming**, plus a methane feedback of 2.8 [0.7 to 7.3] PgC-equivalent per °C ([Chapter 5, Section 5.4.8](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter05.pdf)). AR6 WG1 SPM B.5.2 adds that "loss of permafrost carbon following permafrost thaw is irreversible at centennial time scales (high confidence)" ([WG1 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)).

This is the cleanest "extra emissions forever" element in the whole list, and the only one whose central estimate is below +2 °C.

> *Card line:* The frozen ground gives way, and a thousand billion tonnes of old carbon starts breathing out.

### Greenland Ice Sheet — collapse

**+1.5 °C (0.8–3.0), ten thousand years (minimum ~1,000).** Driven by the melt–elevation feedback: as the surface loses height it drops into warmer air and melts faster. Its own warming feedback is small, **+0.13 °C globally**, but its sea-level commitment is about seven metres. Armstrong McKay note that the collapse "may still be avoidable if GMST returns below 1.5 °C within an uncertain overshoot time (likely decades)".

AR6 is more cautious and does not endorse a threshold this low — it states "limited evidence for irreversible GrIS loss below 3 °C", and AR6 WG1 Chapter 9 says only that at "sustained warming levels between 2 °C and 3 °C ... there is limited evidence that the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets will be lost almost completely and irreversibly over multiple millennia" ([Chapter 9](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter09.pdf)). The AR6 Synthesis Report SPM says the same, "causing several metres of sea level rise (limited evidence)" ([SYR SPM B.3.2](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_SPM.pdf)).

> *Card line:* Greenland's ice passes the point of return; the sea will take seven metres, and it will not give them back.

### West Antarctic Ice Sheet — collapse

**+1.5 °C (1.0–3.0), two thousand years.** Marine ice sheet instability: much of the WAIS is grounded below sea level, and once the grounding line retreats onto a backward slope the retreat sustains itself. Thwaites' grounding line "is only ~30 km away from the subglacial ridge and retreating at ~1 km per year"; models "support irreversible retreat being underway for present levels of ocean warming", and losing Thwaites may destabilise much of the WAIS. Warming feedback **+0.05 °C**; several metres of sea level.

AR6 flags the deep uncertainty here rather than a threshold: sea level rise "approaching 2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150 under a very high GHG emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5) (low confidence) — cannot be ruled out due to deep uncertainty in ice-sheet processes" ([WG1 SPM B.5.3](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). Chapter 9 adds that abrupt marine ice sheet and marine ice cliff instability "could in combination contribute more than one additional metre of sea level rise by 2100", and that by 2300 sea level could rise "by up to 16 m under SSP5-8.5 considering marine ice cliff instability (low confidence)" ([Chapter 9](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter09.pdf)).

Greenland and West Antarctica are usually treated as one event for design purposes: same central estimate, same kind of consequence, and the Global Tipping Points Report 2025 says both "are already nearing their tipping points, and if triggered would commit the world to up to 10 metres of sea level rise in the long-run" ([NOC](https://www.noc.ac.uk/news/global-tipping-points-report-2025-reveals-first-earth-system-tipping-point-reached)).

> *Card line:* Thwaites lets go, and West Antarctica begins a retreat nothing can stop.

### Labrador–Irminger Seas / subpolar gyre convection — collapse

**+1.8 °C (1.1–3.8), ten years — the fastest element in the table.** Deep convection in the North Atlantic subpolar gyre shuts down. Its feedback is **negative: −0.5 °C globally, −3 °C regionally.** It cools north-west Europe hard and fast while the planet keeps warming. A ten-year transition is the only one that would actually be visible inside a human career.

> *Card line:* The North Atlantic stops turning over; Europe freezes while the world burns.

### Mountain glaciers — loss

**+2.0 °C (1.5–3.0), 200 years.** Feedback **+0.08 °C**, plus the water supply of a large fraction of humanity. Armstrong McKay note that extra-polar glacier loss "becomes likely by ~2 °C".

> *Card line:* The mountain ice that feeds the rivers is gone by summer, every summer.

### Sahel and West African monsoon — greening

**+2.8 °C (2.0–3.5), fifty years.** A monsoon shift that greens the Sahel. Counter-intuitively the report treats it as a harm — abrupt regional upheaval regardless of the direction — and it is one of the least certain entries in the table.

> *Card line:* The monsoon jumps north and the Sahara starts to green, and nothing there is where it was.

### East Antarctic subglacial basins — collapse

**+3.0 °C (2.0–6.0), two thousand years.** The Wilkes, Aurora and Recovery basins are also subject to marine ice sheet instability; "one model indicates that Wilkes collapse may be committed by 3 to 4 °C". Feedback assumed similar to WAIS, ~+0.05 °C. This sits right on the Collapse Line and its minimum of 2.0 is inside the game.

> *Card line:* East Antarctica's buried basins start to drain, and the sea has somewhere new to come from.

### Amazon rainforest — dieback

**+3.5 °C (2.0–6.0), 100 years.** Central estimate above the game's range, minimum well inside it. Partial dieback releases **30 GtC (+0.1 °C)**; total dieback **75 GtC (+0.2 °C)**. The Global Tipping Points Report 2025 argues the effective threshold is lower once deforestation is counted: the Amazon "could face widespread dieback below 2 C global warming" when warming and clearing combine ([global-tipping-points.org](https://global-tipping-points.org/), [NOC](https://www.noc.ac.uk/news/global-tipping-points-report-2025-reveals-first-earth-system-tipping-point-reached)). AR6 assesses tropical forest as susceptible to abrupt change with *low* confidence and gives no percentage ([Chapter 4, Table 4.10](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter04.pdf)).

This is the only element whose trigger a player could plausibly *pull directly* rather than by warming the planet — which makes it the most game-shaped one in the list.

> *Card line:* The rainforest stops making its own rain, and the biggest carbon sink on land becomes a source.

### AMOC — collapse

**+4.0 °C (1.4–8.0), fifty years.** The central estimate is far outside the game's range but the minimum, 1.4 °C, is at its very start, and the range is the widest of any element — Armstrong McKay list AMOC among the estimates that are "highly uncertain". Feedback **−0.5 °C globally, −4 to −10 °C regionally**.

AR6 WG1 SPM C.3.4: the AMOC "is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios ... There is medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100" ([WG1 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). Since AR6, van Westen, Kliphuis and Dijkstra (2024) reported a physics-based early warning signal indicating "the present-day AMOC is on route to tipping" ([*Science Advances* 10, eadk1189](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adk1189)), and the Global Tipping Points Report 2025 now says AMOC "is at risk of collapse below 2 °C of global warming" ([NOC](https://www.noc.ac.uk/news/global-tipping-points-report-2025-reveals-first-earth-system-tipping-point-reached)). The direction of travel in the literature is downward.

> *Card line:* The Atlantic conveyor stalls; Europe's winters turn savage and the monsoons wander.

### Boreal permafrost — collapse

**+4.0 °C (3.0–6.0), fifty years.** The "compost bomb": abrupt drying at ~4 °C triggers self-heating in carbon-rich deep permafrost, releasing **125–250 GtC (175–350 GtC-equivalent with methane), worth +0.2 to +0.4 °C.** Outside the game's range but only just — its minimum is exactly the Collapse Line.

> *Card line:* The deep frozen soil catches its own heat and cooks itself from the inside.

## The two things the table does not say outright

**Which are already at risk near +1.5 °C.** The paper's own summary: "Current global warming of ~1.1 °C above pre-industrial already lies within the lower end of five CTP uncertainty ranges", and "Six CTPs become likely (with a further four possible) within the Paris Agreement range of 1.5 to <2 °C warming, including collapse of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, die-off of low-latitude coral reefs, and widespread abrupt permafrost thaw." Reading the minimum column, those five are Greenland (0.8), West Antarctic (1.0), coral reefs (1.0), abrupt permafrost thaw (1.0) and the subpolar gyre (1.1) — that identification is my arithmetic from Table 1, not a list the paper prints. The paper adds that "we cannot rule out that WAIS and GrIS tipping points have already been passed", and concludes that **~1 °C "is a level of global warming that minimizes the likelihood of crossing CTPs"** — below where the game starts.

Going from 1.5 to 2 °C the paper says the likelihood rises for Greenland, West Antarctica, coral die-off and abrupt permafrost thaw, and the best estimate for subpolar gyre collapse is crossed. Going from 2 to 3 °C, "maximum estimated thresholds for abrupt permafrost thaw, GrIS, WAIS, and extra-polar glaciers are passed, suggesting that tipping them would become very likely."

**Which feed back on warming, and which only raise the sea.**

- *Feed back on warming, positively:* abrupt permafrost thaw (+0.04 to +0.11 °C per °C), permafrost collapse (+0.2 to +0.4 °C), Amazon dieback (+0.1 to +0.2 °C), mountain glaciers (+0.08 °C), boreal forest northern expansion (+0.14 °C), Arctic winter sea ice (+0.6 °C), and weakly the ice sheets (+0.05 to +0.13 °C each).
- *Feed back negatively — they cool:* subpolar gyre collapse (−0.5 °C), AMOC collapse (−0.5 °C), boreal forest southern dieback (−0.18 °C net, because losing dark forest brightens the land more than the released carbon warms it).
- *Mostly sea level:* Greenland (~7 m), West Antarctic (several m), East Antarctic subglacial basins, East Antarctic Ice Sheet (~50 m of potential). Their temperature feedbacks are almost negligible; their sea level commitments are the whole story.
- *Neither:* coral reefs, Barents Sea ice, the Sahel monsoon — human and ecological cost, no material climate feedback.

There is also a non-tipping process worth more to this game than most of the tipping elements, because it maps straight onto the Natural Sink. AR6 WG1 Chapter 5 finds "high confidence that the global land sink will become less efficient due to soil moisture limitations and associated drought conditions in some regions for high-emissions scenarios, especially under global warming above 4 °C" ([Chapter 5](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter05.pdf)), and Armstrong McKay note that although not tipping elements, "land carbon sink weakening would become significant by 2 °C" and above 2 °C "land carbon sink-to-source transitions would become widespread". The sink shrinking as the world warms is better established than most of the thresholds above.

**Committed sea level, for calibrating any sea-level rule.** AR6 WG1 SPM B.5.4: "Over the next 2000 years, global mean sea level will rise by about 2 to 3 m if warming is limited to 1.5 °C, 2 to 6 m if limited to 2 °C and 19 to 22 m with 5 °C of warming" ([WG1 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). Chapter 9 gives "about 4 to 10 m with 3 °C of peak warming". So across the game's entire range, +1.2 to +3.0 °C, the committed sea level roughly triples.

## The timescale problem, stated plainly

Nothing in the table happens on a game turn. The fastest element is subpolar gyre collapse at ten years; coral die-off is ten; Arctic winter sea ice twenty; Barents Sea ice twenty-five; then AMOC and permafrost collapse at fifty, Amazon and boreal forest at a hundred, abrupt permafrost thaw at two hundred, and the ice sheets at two thousand to ten thousand.

A Turn in this game is one month, and the First Playable is twenty-four of them — two years of game time across nearly two degrees of warming. The Climate Model is already compressed far past physical realism, which is a fine choice for a strategy game and is not a problem to solve here. It does mean one thing for design, though: **the honest reading of a tipping point in this game is "committed", not "happened".** Crossing the threshold buys the consequence; the physics would deliver it centuries later. Whether the game says so out loud is a presentation decision.

## Suggested game mapping

**This section is a recommendation for the designer, not a decision.** The four below are picked for coverage — one of each effect type named in the ticket — and for having the best-supported numbers. Temperatures, magnitudes and names are all open.

### First, two things to settle before any of this

**A naming collision.** `CONTEXT.md` lists "tipping point" under *Avoid* for **Collapse Line**. Introducing a game term "Tipping Point" would contradict the glossary. Three ways out: rename these to something else (Thresholds, Breaks, Commitments); drop "tipping point" from the Collapse Line avoid-list and let the two coexist; or keep the avoid-list and let these be Events with no collective name. My preference is the first — *Break* reads well on a card and keeps the Collapse Line unambiguous — but this is a glossary decision and belongs to you.

**How big an effect has to be to be felt.** The conversion is +0.5 °C per 90 ppm above 420, so **18 ppm buys 0.1 °C**. Starting Emissions are about 14 ppm a turn (6.0 from Nation State industry, ~8 from population) against a 6.0 Natural Sink, so a net of roughly 8 ppm a turn. A Break that fires at +2.0 °C lands somewhere around turn 10 to 14, leaving eight to fourteen turns. To be worth a tenth of a degree by the last turn it therefore has to be worth **at least 1.5 to 2.5 ppm a turn** — anything smaller is invisible. That arithmetic is mine, from `docs/spec/first-playable.md` §11.1–11.2 and `docs/spec/version-0.02.md`; the numbers below are sized to it, and every one of them is a knob.

### The four

**1. Permafrost Thaw — at +1.5 °C — extra Emissions, permanently**

The best-supported element under +2 °C, and the only one whose real effect is straightforwardly "more carbon every year from now on". Suggested effect: **+2.5 ppm to this turn's Emissions and every turn after, as its own line on the Climate Panel.** It is not attributable to either Faction, which means it should probably not count against the Custodians' Stabilization run — the same exemption the existing Permafrost Thaw Event card already has.

Note the overlap: there is **already** a Climate Event card called Permafrost Thaw (+3.0 Emissions next turn, scaled by Temperature) in `docs/spec/version-0.02.md`. Either the Break needs a different name (Thermokarst? The Ground Gives Way?) or the card does. Worth deciding rather than shipping two.

*Card line:* The frozen ground gives way, and a thousand billion tonnes of old carbon starts breathing out.

**2. The Sink Weakens — at +2.0 °C — a smaller Natural Sink, permanently**

Maps AR6's high-confidence finding about the land sink onto the game's cleanest lever. Suggested effect: **the Natural Sink drops from 6.0 to 4.0 ppm a turn, permanently.** That is worth 2.0 ppm a turn, right at the threshold of being felt, and it is brutal for the Custodians specifically — their Victory Condition is net Emissions under the Sink for three consecutive turns, and this moves the bar without them doing anything wrong. That asymmetry is either the best thing about this Break or a reason to reject it; your call.

Strictly this is a feedback, not a tipping point. It is in the list because it is better established than most of the real thresholds and because it is the only natural fit for the Natural Sink lever.

*Card line:* The forests and the oceans are full; the world stops taking back what we give it.

**3. Ice Sheets Committed — at +2.2 °C — an extra Sea Level threshold**

Greenland and West Antarctica together, treated as one event as the literature effectively does. Suggested effect: **fires a Sea Level threshold immediately on crossing, out of sequence** — every Nation State loses build slots equal to its Coastal Exposure, exactly as +1.8, +2.3 and +2.8 already do, and the three scheduled thresholds still fire on their own. So a game that reaches +2.8 has taken four hits rather than three.

At +2.2 this lands between the existing +1.8 and +2.3 thresholds and stacks with the +2.3 almost immediately, which is a hard, legible punishment for letting the Temperature run. If that is too punishing, +2.5 spreads them out. This is the one Break whose effect does not depend on how many turns are left, which makes it the most reliable of the four.

*Card line:* Greenland and Thwaites pass the point of return; the sea is coming for the coasts and it will not stop.

**4. Amazon Dieback — at +2.6 °C — a Nation State's Baseline Emissions raised**

The only element a player can push toward directly, and the only one that is *somewhere* rather than everywhere. Suggested effect: **South America's Baseline Emissions rise from 0.2 to 1.2, permanently**, plus a one-off **+15 ppm to the CO2 Stock** for the dieback pulse itself. At South America's starting Industry Level of 1 the ongoing part is only +1.0 ppm a turn, rising to +3.0 if someone has built it up to Level 3 — which is the point: the Faction that industrialised South America pays most for burning it.

The one-off pulse is scaled from the paper's 30 GtC partial-dieback figure, which is roughly 14 ppm of atmospheric CO2 at the standard 2.13 GtC per ppm. That conversion is mine, not the paper's.

If you would rather it not single out one state, the alternative is raising every state's Baseline Emissions by 0.2, which is +1.8 ppm a turn at Industry Level 1 across nine states and hits everyone equally.

*Card line:* The rainforest stops making its own rain, and the biggest carbon sink on land becomes a source.

### Two candidates deliberately left out

**Warm-water coral die-off** is the most defensible tipping point in the whole list — it is being crossed right now at +1.4 °C, which is inside the game's opening turns — but **it has no climate feedback at all**, so it cannot be expressed in any of the four levers. If you want it in, its honest effect is on people, not on carbon: population, or a Nation State's Standing, or the loss of a coastal build slot. It would make a good *early* Break, the one that fires almost immediately and tells the player what these are, precisely because it does not accelerate the game.

**AMOC collapse** is the most famous of them and it *cools*. A Break that lowered the Temperature would be a reward for failing, which is backwards. If it goes in, its effect belongs somewhere other than the Climate Model — Europe and North America losing population or output while the Temperature carries on rising.
